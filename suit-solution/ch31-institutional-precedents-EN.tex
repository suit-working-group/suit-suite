\chapter{Institutional precedents}
\label{ch:institutional-precedents}

\begin{caveatbox}[Status of this chapter]
\noindent The institutional precedents in this chapter are
identified as starting points for the institution's own research,
not as direct templates. The architectural choices, operational
practices, and contractual arrangements at any cited institution
should be confirmed against that institution's own current public
documentation before being cited as precedent in derivative work.
This chapter records the institutions that have appeared in
public discussion of the patterns this Reference draws on; it
does not claim independent verification of every aspect of every
deployment.
\end{caveatbox}

The architecture and playbook of this Reference do not invent
their structural patterns from first principles --- they
consolidate patterns visible at named institutions whose practices
have been the subject of public discussion. This chapter records
the institutions that have appeared most consistently as
references, organised by region and by the practice they
illustrate. The catalogue is intended for the international
university network anticipated in phase~2 of this initiative as a
starting point that the network will refine and extend with
direct contributions from each member.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Method}
\label{sec:ip-method}

The institutions below are identified through three methodological
filters: appearance in NREN community fora and at EDUCAUSE in
recent publications; reference in NIST or ISO/IEC publications
that draw on academic deployments; mention in publicly available
research-network case material (GÉANT, ESnet, Internet2 case
studies). The filters bias the catalogue toward institutions that
publish their architectures --- in practice, larger and flagship
institutions that maintain public architecture documentation;
institutions whose practice is mature but undocumented, and smaller or
teaching-led institutions that consume the same primitives as shared
services rather than documenting their own builds, are necessarily
under-represented. This is a documentation bias, not a statement about
which institutions the architecture can serve (see
\S\ref{sec:ip-beyond-flagship}).

\paragraph{What is and is not claimed.} The catalogue claims that
the named institution has been the subject of public discussion of
the practice attributed to it. The catalogue does \emph{not} claim
that the institution's current architecture matches the description;
that the institution endorses the pattern's use in this Reference;
or that the cited practice is the institution's authoritative
position. Each entry should be treated as a starting point for the
reader's own research.

\paragraph{Refresh cadence.} The catalogue would benefit from
annual review: institutional architectures evolve, public
documentation is revised or withdrawn, NREN community discussions
move forward. The phase-2 international network is the natural
locus for this refresh.

\section{European institutions}
\label{sec:ip-europe}

\paragraph{\institution{CERN} (Geneva).} The European
Organisation for Nuclear Research has been the subject of
extensive public discussion of its identity federation, audit
pipeline, and high-performance experimental network architecture.
The migration of CERN's identity and access management from
Microsoft ADFS to a Keycloak-based open-source SSO is documented
in conference proceedings~\cite{cern-keycloak-2020}. Its scale
(multi-institutional collaboration spanning many countries) and
its public-documentation practice make it one of the most
frequently cited research-organisation references for
research-intensive university IT.

\paragraph{\institution{ETH Z\"urich}.} The Federal Institute of
Technology in Z\"urich has been cited for its model of granting
local administrator rights through a documented procedure
(request, ISG risk assessment, approval by an authorised person
in the applicant's organisational unit) and for its ISG (IT
Services Group) federated organisation across departments. The
pattern of distributed IT authority within a coherent institutional
framework is a recurring point of reference in this Reference's
federated-governance discussions.

\paragraph{\institution{University of Cambridge}.} The University
Information Services (UIS) operates the central technology
function for the Collegiate University, with the University Data
Network (UDN) as the institutional backbone connecting the
central services and the colleges. The collegiate model
illustrates a federated-network pattern under a coherent
institutional architecture.

\paragraph{\institution{University of Oxford}.} Distributed IT
under a central InfoSec policy framework has been the subject of
public material. The pattern is similar to Cambridge in spirit but
differs in operational details that the institution's own
documentation should be consulted for.

\paragraph{\institution{KU Leuven}.} The Belgian flagship
research-intensive university operates ICTS as the central IT
function and is one of the European reference institutions for
research-data infrastructure. Public documentation is more
limited than for the larger NREN-anchored institutions.

\section{North American institutions}
\label{sec:ip-north-america}

\paragraph{\institution{Massachusetts Institute of Technology}
(MIT).} MIT IS\&T (Information Systems and Technology), the
Information Technology Governance Committee (ITGC) reporting to
the Provost and Executive Vice President, and the Computer
Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory's TIG (Technical
Infrastructure Group) appear recurrently as references for the
federated-IT-governance pattern. The ITGC operates with a set
of related committees (Infrastructure Steering, Policy, Project)
that consult on different aspects of IT strategy. Public
documentation is extensive and accessible.

\paragraph{\institution{Stanford University}.} Stanford
University Network (SUNet), University IT (UIT), and the
Stanford Research Computing Center (SRCC) appear as references
for large-scale research-intensive university IT. The public
documentation of the multi-zone segmentation pattern is a
frequently cited starting point.

\paragraph{\institution{University of Michigan}.} ITS, the
Information Technology Council, and the Merit Network
relationship illustrate the NREN-anchored pattern at a large
research-intensive university. The College of Engineering's
CAEN (Computer-Aided Engineering Network) is an instance of
faculty-level IT autonomy under institutional governance.

\paragraph{\institution{Indiana University}.} The Jetstream2
research-cloud architecture~\cite{jetstream2-2021}, operated under
the U.S.~National Science Foundation's ACCESS programme, is one of
the most extensively documented research-cloud deployments. It is
cited recurrently in this Reference's discussion of compute and
research-storage patterns.

\paragraph{\institution{University of Toronto}.} The Canadian
research-intensive university is frequently cited for its
identity-federation participation and for the integration of
faculty IT with central services. Public documentation is
accessible through the institution's IT services pages.

\section{Asia-Pacific institutions}
\label{sec:ip-asia-pacific}

\paragraph{\institution{National University of Singapore}.} NUS
has been cited for federation patterns at the regional scale and
for the integration of multi-jurisdiction research collaborations.
Public documentation is partial; English-language material is
more accessible than for some other regional references.

\paragraph{\institution{Australian National University} (ANU).}
ANU's participation in the Australian Access Federation (AAF) and
in AARNet-anchored research networking is a publicly documented
reference for Asia-Pacific research-network patterns.

\paragraph{\institution{Tsinghua University}.} The Beijing
research-intensive university is one of the most-cited Chinese
references; English-language public documentation is more
limited than for the European and North American institutions
listed above. Direct engagement with the institution would be
necessary for any precedent claim.

\section{Latin American institutions}
\label{sec:ip-latin-america}

\paragraph{\institution{Universidade de S\~ao Paulo} (USP).} The
Brazilian flagship research university is one of the principal
references for Latin American research-network patterns. Its
participation in the Rede Nacional de Ensino e Pesquisa (RNP) and
in the CAFe federation provides documented federation precedent.

\paragraph{\institution{Universidad Nacional Aut\'onoma de
M\'exico} (UNAM).} The Mexican flagship public research
university is a reference for large-scale public-university IT.
Public documentation in Spanish is extensive; English-language
material is more limited.

\section{NRENs and consortium initiatives}
\label{sec:ip-nrens}

National Research and Education Networks and their consortia are
the principal multipliers of the patterns this Reference
develops. They produce shared documentation, operate shared
services, and host community fora in which architectural
discussions take place.

\paragraph{\gls{geant}.} The pan-European research-network
backbone connects the European NRENs and operates services
(eduGAIN~\cite{edugain}, eduTEAMS~\cite{eduteams}, eduroam) on which
the Reference's identity and attachment patterns rest. The GÉANT community fora are a
principal venue for the discussions feeding this Reference.

\paragraph{\institution{RESTENA} (Luxembourg).} The Luxembourg
NREN connects the country's research and education community to
GÉANT and operates institutional services under the
RESTENA-CSIRT umbrella. RESTENA's role as a shared operator is
relevant to several of the architecture's mutualisation
patterns.

\paragraph{Internet2 (United States).} The U.S.~research-network
consortium operates services analogous to GÉANT's and is the
principal North American venue for community discussions of
architectural patterns at research-intensive universities.

\paragraph{\institution{JISC} (United Kingdom).} The UK
research-and-education service organisation operates Janet, the
UK NREN, and supports a large body of community guidance on
which the Reference's patterns draw.

\paragraph{\institution{RNP} (Brazil).} The Rede Nacional de
Ensino e Pesquisa is the principal Latin American NREN and
operates the CAFe identity federation.

\paragraph{\institution{AARNet} (Australia).} The Australian
academic network and the AAF identity federation provide the
Asia-Pacific reference for the patterns this Reference draws on.

\section{The precedents illustrate the architecture; they do not gate it}
\label{sec:ip-beyond-flagship}

The catalogue above is dominated by large, research-intensive
institutions for the methodological reason stated in
\S\ref{sec:ip-method}: such institutions maintain extensive public
architecture documentation, so their practice is citable. This must
not be read as an admissibility bar. As established in
\trchap{ch:architectural-principles}{} (\S\ref{sec:ap-necessity}), the
functions the architecture discharges are a minimal necessary set for
any institution offering a digital infrastructure, several of them
legally compelled regardless of size; the precedents illustrate
\emph{how} those functions are realised at scale, they do not define
\emph{which} institutions the architecture serves.

\paragraph{The shared-service channel.} A national
research-and-education network exists to serve its country's entire
research and education community, not its flagship institutions alone:
\gls{restena} in Luxembourg~\cite{restena}, DFN in Germany~\cite{dfn}
and SWITCH in Switzerland~\cite{switch-nren} are mandated accordingly.
Through such operators, federated identity
(\gls{edugain}~\cite{edugain}), collaborative identity
(eduTEAMS~\cite{eduteams}) and federated network access (eduroam) are
available to a small or teaching-led institution as managed services,
without that institution operating the underlying infrastructure;
\gls{geant}~\cite{geant} carries the same primitives across borders. A
precedent need not be a flagship to be relevant: an institution that
consumes federated identity through its NREN is exercising exactly the
identity-layer pattern of \trchap{ch:wave1-identity}{}, at a
realisation it can sustain.

\paragraph{Implication for the catalogue.} The phase-2 international
network (\S\ref{sec:ip-method}) is the natural locus for recording
such adoptions explicitly and broadening the catalogue beyond the
publication-rich flagships that necessarily dominate it today;
sector-guidance bodies such as \institution{JISC}~\cite{jisc-guidance}
already document these patterns for institutions across the size range.

\section{Documented gaps}
\label{sec:ip-gaps}

Several areas of the architecture remain thinly documented in
public institutional sources:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Policy plane deployments at academic scale.} The
    industrial OPA and Cedar deployments provide most public
    documentation; academic deployments are reported informally
    in NREN fora but are rarely the subject of detailed public
    architecture documents.
  \item \textbf{TAC-aware audit pipelines.} The canonical TAC
    schema and the cross-layer correlation pattern are not, at
    the time of writing, the subject of any single publicly
    documented academic deployment. Various aspects are visible
    at various institutions; the consolidated pattern is novel
    to this Reference.
  \item \textbf{Sovereignty grid practice.} The grid is novel to
    this Reference. Adjacent practice exists (procurement
    sovereignty clauses, exit-data-export contractual language)
    but a structured five-dimension grid applied across an
    institutional IT portfolio has not, to the author's
    knowledge, been the subject of public institutional
    documentation.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The phase-2 international university network is the
locus where these gaps would be addressed: institutions that
adopt the architecture would publish their experience, and the
catalogue of precedents would acquire entries that this version
cannot yet record.
